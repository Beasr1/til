# 11. Key attestation, field by field

Assumes [file 10](10-hardware-backed-attestation.md). Chains in general — what an issuer CA is,
why path building and path validation are different jobs — are in the
[`pki`](../pki/README.md) course; this chapter assumes [`pki/01`](../pki/01-what-a-certificate-is.md)
and leans on [`pki/03` §3.3](../pki/03-four-chains-compared.md) for where attestation sits
among other chains.

## The problem

File 10 said the secure environment returns "a certificate chain" carrying "an attestation
extension describing the key and the device". That sentence is correct and it is also where
all the difficulty lives. Two certificates with identical-looking chains can support
completely different conclusions, depending on which of two lists a field appeared in.

This is also the chapter that settles a question people arrive with: **can attestation tell me
my app wasn't decompiled, modified and rebuilt?** The answer is a qualified yes, it rests
entirely on one field, that field is in the weaker of the two lists, and Google's own
verification library does not check it unless you tell it to. Every clause there matters.

## 11.1 What actually comes back

`KeyStore.getCertificateChain(alias)` on a key generated with an attestation challenge returns
an ordinary `X509Certificate` array. Nothing about the API is special.

| Position | Certificate | Carries |
|---|---|---|
| `[0]` leaf | generated by KeyMint for *your* key | the attested public key, and **the attestation extension** |
| `[1..n-1]` | intermediate(s) | the chain upward. With RKP, short-lived |
| `[n-1]` root | Google's attestation root | the anchor — which you supply yourself, and do not take from the chain |

The first surprise is that the leaf's familiar X.509 fields are deliberately empty of
information. AOSP specifies `subject` as the constant string `CN=Android Keystore Key` and
`serialNumber` as the integer `1` on every attestation certificate ever issued
([AOSP, Key and ID attestation](https://source.android.com/docs/security/features/keystore/attestation)).

> ⚠️ A verifier written for TLS habits will reach for the subject name to work out *who* this
> is. There is nothing there, and there was never meant to be. Attestation answers "where does
> this key live?", not "who is this?" ([`pki/03` §3.3](../pki/03-four-chains-compared.md)). The
> payload is in an extension.

## 11.2 Where the chain terminates

The anchor is Google's, published as JSON at `https://android.googleapis.com/attestation/root`.
Fetched October 2026, that endpoint returns **two** roots:

| Root | Key | Validity | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| `SERIALNUMBER=f92009e853b6b045` | RSA 4096 | 2022-03-20 → 2042-03-15 | the long-standing root |
| `CN=Key Attestation CA1, O=Google LLC` | ECDSA P-384 | 2025-07-17 → 2035-07-15 | newer; Google's docs schedule it to begin issuing 1 February 2026 |

Both are self-signed (subject equals issuer), so both are genuinely roots rather than
intermediates — I fetched and parsed that endpoint rather than taking a doc's word for it, and
it's worth doing yourself, because secondary write-ups of this get the details wrong. Two
consequences for anyone verifying today: **hold both**, and don't write code that assumes the
root's key is RSA.

Expiry behaves oddly here, and the oddity is correct. Google's guidance is that devices
launched before 2021 carry attestation keys whose certificates have *expired*, and that these
"are still trustworthy unless they appear in the certificate revocation list" — recommending
you "continue trusting certificates that chain to the root with the subject
`SERIALNUMBER=f92009e853b6b045`, regardless of certificate validity period"
([developer.android.com, Verifying hardware-backed key pairs](https://developer.android.com/privacy-and-security/security-key-attestation)).
That is the anchors-are-configuration rule from [`pki/01` §1.6](../pki/01-what-a-certificate-is.md)
with real money on it — and the same instinct that made Android ignore `notAfter` on TLS trust
anchors.

**But the exception stops at the root.** For RKP chains the validity period *is* load-bearing:
Google is explicit that "the shorter expiration period for RKP certificates is an important
consideration of the threat model, and is intended to allow for quicker responses to
compromises". RKP certificates "will generally be valid for up to two months before they expire
and are rotated"
([Android Developers Blog, March 2022](https://android-developers.googleblog.com/2022/03/upgrading-android-attestation-remote.html)).

### Factory keys versus RKP, and why you care

| | **Factory provisioned** | **Remote Key Provisioning (RKP)** |
|---|---|---|
| Where the attestation key came from | burned in at the factory, in batches | the device generates it; Google certifies the public half over the network |
| Shared across | up to ~100k devices per batch key | **per app**, and rotated |
| Certificate life | years | up to ~two months |
| Leak blast radius | every device sharing that batch key | one app's key, briefly |
| Required | — | optional in Android 12, mandated in 13, **the only mechanism** on devices launching with Android 16 |

This is the fix for the keybox problem. A leaked private attestation key lets an attacker
produce chains that verify perfectly for keys that never went near secure hardware. Factory
keyboxes have leaked both ways — OEMs storing them somewhere readable, and TEE vulnerabilities.

How real is the second route? *Trust Dies in Darkness: Shedding Light on Samsung's TrustZone
Keymaster Design*, Shakevsky, Ronen and Wool (Tel-Aviv University), 31st USENIX Security
Symposium 2022, p.251 — [free PDF](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec22-shakevsky.pdf).
From the abstract: they "present an IV reuse attack on AES-GCM that allows an attacker to
extract hardware-protected key material, and a downgrade attack that makes even the latest
Samsung devices vulnerable", across Galaxy S8 through S21, demonstrating "a working FIDO2
WebAuthn login bypass and a compromise of Google's Secure Key Import".

Be precise about what that does and doesn't show, because it gets over-cited. It is a break of
**hardware-backed key protection** — keys that were supposed to be unextractable came out — not
itself a report of attestation keyboxes being leaked. Its relevance here is the premise, not the
incident: the TEE's "root cannot reach this" guarantee is an engineering claim about a specific
vendor implementation, and specific implementations have failed. File 10 §10.5 said the TEE is
not unbreakable; this is the citation for that sentence.

Google's own framing of the provisioning fix: attestation key leaks "only affect the older
provisioning mechanism supported in Android and don't apply to attestation keys certified by
the newer Remote Key Provisioning (RKP) mechanism."

Google's verifier exposes which mechanism a chain used, so you can require RKP as policy —
`ProvisioningMethod` is `UNKNOWN`, `FACTORY_PROVISIONED` or `REMOTELY_PROVISIONED`
(`provider/KeyAttestationCertPath.kt:167`).

## 11.3 The extension

OID **`1.3.6.1.4.1.11129.2.1.17`**, non-critical, holding a DER `KeyDescription` of eight
fields:

```
KeyDescription ::= SEQUENCE {
    attestationVersion         INTEGER,
    attestationSecurityLevel   SecurityLevel,     -- Software | TrustedEnvironment | StrongBox
    keyMintVersion             INTEGER,
    keyMintSecurityLevel       SecurityLevel,
    attestationChallenge       OCTET_STRING,      -- YOUR nonce
    uniqueId                   OCTET_STRING,
    softwareEnforced           AuthorizationList, -- asserted by Android
    hardwareEnforced           AuthorizationList, -- asserted by the TEE/StrongBox
}
```

`attestationVersion` tells you which schema you're reading: 1–4 are Keymaster 2.0 through 4.1;
100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 are KeyMint 1.0 through 5.0. The field you most want,
`attestationApplicationId`, is "present only in key attestation versions >= 2".

> ⚠️ **Only the first attestation extension in the chain counts.** Google's wording:
> "Only the first occurrence of the extension in the chain can be trusted. Any further
> instances of the extension have not been issued by the secure hardware and might have been
> issued by an attacker extending the chain while attempting to create fake attestations for
> untrusted keys." A verifier that scans the chain for "the certificate with the extension" and
> takes the last match it finds can be fed a forged one.

## 11.4 The two authorisation lists — the hinge of the whole chapter

`softwareEnforced` and `hardwareEnforced` have the **same ASN.1 type**, so any tag may
*syntactically* appear in either. What differs is who is asserting it, and AOSP defines that
precisely:

| List | AOSP's definition |
|---|---|
| `hardwareEnforced` | "enforced by the device's Trusted Execution Environment (TEE) or StrongBox. This information is collected or generated by code in the secure hardware and **is not controlled by the platform**" |
| `softwareEnforced` | "enforced by the Android system. This information is collected or generated by code in the platform. It **can be trusted as long as the device is running an operating system that complies with the Android Platform Security Model**" |

Read the last clause of the second row slowly, because it is a conditional, and the condition
is checkable. A software-enforced field is not worthless and it is not hardware-grade. It is
**trustworthy exactly to the extent that you have independently established that the OS is
genuine** — which is precisely what the hardware-enforced `RootOfTrust` tells you.

So the two lists are not two grades of evidence sitting side by side. They are a chain of
reasoning, and it only runs in one direction:

```
  hardwareEnforced.rootOfTrust says:  verifiedBootState = Verified,  deviceLocked = true
                                            │
                                            │  so the booted OS is an OEM-signed image
                                            ▼
                      …so the Android platform code is genuine AOSP/OEM code
                                            │
                                            │  and that code is what fills in softwareEnforced
                                            ▼
            …so softwareEnforced.attestationApplicationId can be believed
```

Break the top and the bottom is worth nothing. An unlocked bootloader running a modified OS
means the platform code filling in `attestationApplicationId` is code the attacker controls —
and the TEE will faithfully sign whatever that code told it.

> **Teacher's aside.** This is the most mis-taught thing about attestation, and the mistake has
> a satisfying shape: people see that the whole blob is signed by the TEE and conclude the whole
> blob is hardware-guaranteed. The signature is genuine. What the signature attests is "keystore
> told me this" — the TEE is vouching for the *provenance of the statement*, not auditing its
> contents. A TEE has no way to independently determine which app is calling it; it is handed a
> package name and some digests by the Android keystore daemon, and it signs them.
>
> Which gives you the one sentence to carry out of this chapter: **a hardware signature over a
> software-asserted field proves the software said it, not that it's true.** And the reason the
> scheme still works is that the same signed blob carries a hardware-enforced field that tells
> you whether that software was trustworthy. You must read both, and you must read them in
> order. Checking `attestationApplicationId` while ignoring `RootOfTrust` is not a weaker
> version of the check — it's no check at all.

Placement is not left to the vendor. The KeyMint HAL's `Tag.aidl` states the requirement per
tag, and the wording is unambiguous
([AOSP `hardware/interfaces/security/keymint/aidl/.../Tag.aidl`](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/hardware/interfaces/+/refs/heads/main/security/keymint/aidl/android/hardware/security/keymint/Tag.aidl)):

| Tag | The HAL says | So it lands in | And Google's verifier reads it from |
|---|---|---|---|
| `ATTESTATION_APPLICATION_ID` [709] | **"Cannot be hardware-enforced."** | `softwareEnforced` | `softwareEnforced` — `ConstraintConfig.kt:407`, `413` |
| `ORIGIN` [702] | **"Must be hardware-enforced."** | `hardwareEnforced` | `hardwareEnforced` — `ConstraintConfig.kt:74` |
| `OS_VERSION` [705] | **"Must be hardware-enforced."** | `hardwareEnforced` | `hardwareEnforced` — `ConstraintConfig.kt:408` |
| `OS_PATCHLEVEL` [706] | **"Must be hardware-enforced."** | `hardwareEnforced` | — |
| `ROOT_OF_TRUST` [704] | "Must never appear in `KeyCharacteristics`" — it exists only for attestation records | `hardwareEnforced` | `hardwareEnforced` — `Verifier.kt:345`, required non-null at `ConstraintConfig.kt:79` |

(Verifier line references are [`github.com/android/keyattestation`](https://github.com/android/keyattestation),
commit `621149c`, 13 October 2026. The spec and the implementation agree, which is the
reassuring outcome — but it is the *spec* that makes this a rule rather than an observation.)

`ATTESTATION_APPLICATION_ID` "cannot be hardware-enforced" is about as direct as a HAL gets.
The same file points at `keystore_attestation_id.cpp` for how the value is constructed —
confirming §11.4's whole argument from the platform side: Android builds it, KeyMint signs it.

Note `origin` must be `Origin.GENERATED` — the key was *created* in the secure environment, not
imported into it. An imported key proves nothing about where it has been.

> ⚠️ One honest wrinkle in "hardware-enforced". `OS_VERSION` and `OS_PATCHLEVEL` are mandated
> hardware-enforced, but the HAL "must read the current OS version from the system property
> `ro.build.version.release` and deliver it to the secure environment when the HAL is first
> loaded". So the TEE *enforces* the value — it won't let it change afterwards — but the value
> originated in the normal world at HAL load. That's still meaningfully stronger than
> `softwareEnforced`, and it's weaker than `rootOfTrust`, which the secure environment measures
> during boot rather than being told. "Hardware-enforced" names who guards a value, which is
> not always the same as who determined it.

## 11.5 `attestationApplicationId` — the app identity field

This is the field that answers the repackaging question. Its contents:

```
AttestationApplicationId ::= SEQUENCE {
    package_infos      SET OF AttestationPackageInfo,
    signature_digests  SET OF OCTET_STRING,   -- SHA-256 of each signing cert
}

AttestationPackageInfo ::= SEQUENCE {
    package_name   OCTET_STRING,
    version        INTEGER,
}
```

AOSP describes it as reflecting "the Android platform's belief as to which apps are allowed to
use the secret key material under attestation", and `signature_digests` as "a set of SHA-256
digests of the app's signing certificates" — the leaf of each signing chain. It can list several
packages only when they share a UID.

**Where your expected digest comes from** is the step people get wrong, because of Play App
Signing:

| You have | Is it the digest you compare against? |
|---|---|
| your **upload** key's certificate | **No.** It authenticates uploads to Play; Google re-signs |
| the **app signing** key's certificate (Play Console → Setup → App signing) | **Yes** — this is what the installed APK is signed with |
| your own release keystore, if you don't use Play App Signing | Yes |

Get this wrong and every genuine device fails. It's a five-minute bug that reads like a
platform problem.

**How Google's verifier matches** is worth knowing because it's looser than "equals", and the
looseness is deliberate (`Extension.kt:667–678`, `724–726`):

- for packages: **any** expected package name matches, and the candidate's `version` must be
  **≥** your expected version — a minimum-version floor, so you can refuse downgrades;
- for signatures: **any** expected digest must appear in the candidate's set — so a key rotation
  can be handled by listing both digests;
- a candidate carrying *extra* digests still passes.

> ⚠️ **Two ways to pass this check vacuously**, both of which I'd expect to see in production
> code. The strictest named constraint is
> `STRICT: { expectedId, actualId, _ -> expectedId?.isSatisfiedBy(actualId) ?: true }`
> (`ConstraintConfig.kt:428–431`) — if `expectedId` is **null**, it returns **true**. And
> `expectedAttestationApplicationId` is a `Verifier` constructor parameter that
> **defaults to null** (`Verifier.kt:155`). Separately, an expected ID with empty `packages`
> *and* empty `signatures` satisfies `isSatisfiedBy` unconditionally. So a verifier can be
> configured with the strictest policy available, log "attestation verified", and never have
> compared an app identity at all. The library's default is looser still —
> `ALLOW_UNKNOWN_PACKAGE`, which additionally lets devices at or below OS version 35 pass while
> reporting the literal package name `UnknownPackage`.
>
> The README is also inconsistent on this point — its example passes `expectedAppId` where
> `ALLOW_UNKNOWN_PACKAGE` takes a `BigInteger` max-OS-version. Read the source, not the snippet.

## 11.6 `RootOfTrust` — the hardware-enforced half

```
RootOfTrust ::= SEQUENCE {
    verifiedBootKey    OCTET_STRING,       -- hash of the key that verified boot
    deviceLocked       BOOLEAN,
    verifiedBootState  VerifiedBootState,
    verifiedBootHash   OCTET_STRING,       -- optional; digest of AVB-protected data
}
```

`verifiedBootState` is [file 05](05-the-boot-chain-and-verified-boot.md)'s lock states, named:

| `verifiedBootState` | File 05's colour | Means |
|---|---|---|
| `Verified` | GREEN | full chain of trust to the factory-embedded root; `verifiedBootKey` is the hash of that root |
| `SelfSigned` | YELLOW | verified against a **user-configured** root — a custom OS, correctly signed by someone who isn't the OEM |
| `Unverified` | ORANGE | **bootloader unlocked**; `verifiedBootKey` is 32 zero bytes |
| `Failed` | RED | verification failed |

`deviceLocked` is specified as true when "the device booted a signed image that was successfully
verified by Verified Boot". Requiring `Verified` **and** `deviceLocked` is the condition that
makes §11.4's reasoning chain run.

## 11.7 The server-side check order

The order below is Google's verifier's, read off `Verifier.kt`. Checks 1–3 are generic chain
work ([`pki/02` §2.2](../pki/02-building-and-walking-a-chain.md)); 4 onward are attestation's own.

| # | Check | If you skip it |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | **Do this on a server, not the device** | "If the Android system on that device is compromised, that could cause the validation process to trust something that is untrustworthy" — and you're back in file 09 |
| 1 | path-validate the chain to a **Google root you hold independently** | an attacker's self-made chain verifies |
| 2 | every serial in the chain against the revocation list (§11.8) | known-leaked keys verify |
| 3 | for RKP chains, validity periods | the short-expiry threat model stops working |
| 4 | parse the extension from the **leaf** — the first occurrence only | an appended forged extension is read |
| 5 | `attestationChallenge` equals the fresh nonce you issued, unseen before | replay of an old attestation |
| 6 | `origin` = `GENERATED` in `hardwareEnforced` | an imported key passes as hardware-born |
| 7 | security level is not `SOFTWARE`, and `attestationSecurityLevel` == `keyMintSecurityLevel` | a software-only attestation passes |
| 8 | `hardwareEnforced.rootOfTrust` present, `verifiedBootState` = `Verified`, `deviceLocked` = true | §11.4's reasoning chain is broken, so check 9 means nothing |
| 9 | `softwareEnforced.attestationApplicationId` matches your expected package **and** signing digest | **§11.9** — the relay attack |

Two details from the implementation worth copying. The reported security level is
`minOf(attestationSecurityLevel, keyMintSecurityLevel)` (`Verifier.kt:342`) — take the weaker of
the two claims, never the stronger. And there is a constraint requiring authorisation-list tags
to be in ascending order (`ConstraintConfig.kt:331–339`), which is a DER conformance check
against malleability of the signed bytes — the
[`smartcards/07`](../smartcards/07-verifying-the-card-certificate.md) encoding war story, in a
different costume.

## 11.8 Revocation

`https://android.googleapis.com/attestation/status` returns JSON keyed by certificate serial in
lowercase hex. Google: "it's critical that the status of each certificate in an attestation
chain be checked against the official certificate revocation status list". Honour the
`Cache-Control` header rather than fetching per verification.

| Field | Values |
|---|---|
| `status` (required) | `REVOKED`, `SUSPENDED` |
| `reason` | `UNSPECIFIED`, `KEY_COMPROMISE`, `CA_COMPROMISE`, `SUPERSEDED`, `SOFTWARE_FLAW` |
| `expires`, `comment` | optional |

What's actually in it, fetched 15 October 2026: **1,761 entries, 180 KB**, served with
`cache-control: public, max-age=86400`. Every entry was `REVOKED` — 1,735 with reason
`KEY_COMPROMISE` and 26 `SOFTWARE_FLAW`; **no `SUSPENDED` entries at all** on that day.

Two things to take from those numbers. The day's cache lifetime tells you the refresh cadence
Google expects: daily, not per-verification. And 1,735 keys revoked for compromise is the
keybox problem in one figure — this is not a theoretical concern, it is a standing list.

> ⚠️ Google's own default revocation source filters on `it.status == "REVOKED"`
> (`GoogleRevocationList.kt:86`), so a serial marked **`SUSPENDED` would pass the stock check**.
> Today that is latent rather than live — there were no `SUSPENDED` entries when I looked — but
> the schema permits them, so the behaviour can change without your code changing. Whether
> treating `SUSPENDED` as acceptable is right is a policy call: `SUSPENDED` with reason
> `SOFTWARE_FLAW` is a malfunctioning key rather than a stolen one. It should be *your* call,
> made deliberately, not one you inherit from a default. The parser does fail closed if the
> list won't parse, which is the correct direction.

## 11.9 So: does it prove the app wasn't repackaged?

Here is the honest answer, in the order the reasoning actually goes.

**What the signing-digest check genuinely catches.** Repackaging requires re-signing (file 03,
file 07) — the attacker has your certificate but not your private key, so a rebuilt APK carries
*their* signing certificate. Its SHA-256 digest differs, the platform reports the different
digest, and check 9 fails. That is a real and valuable detection, and it is the main reason to
do this.

**What it does not do.** Four things, and none is a technicality:

| Claim | Reality |
|---|---|
| "proves the APK bytes are unmodified" | No. It compares a **signing identity**, not a hash of your code. Anything signed by the same key passes — including your own debug build, or a modified build you signed yourself |
| "proves the running process isn't hooked" | No. Frida on an unmodified, correctly-signed app changes nothing in the attestation (file 08). Static detection, not dynamic |
| "is hardware-guaranteed" | No — it's software-enforced (§11.4). It is only as good as the boot state you checked alongside it |
| "proves the *app in front of you* holds the key" | No. The attestation proves a key with those properties exists somewhere. Binding it to this session needs the challenge, and the key must actually be used |

**And the attack that makes check 9 mandatory rather than nice-to-have.** Quarkslab published a
bypass on 11 August 2026 that forges nothing at all:

> "We do not attack the secure hardware, we do not extract keys, and we do not need a leaked
> keybox."

They intercept `generateAttestedKey()` on a rooted phone and relay it to a *clean, locked*
device. Every certificate returned is authentic; `deviceLocked` is genuinely true and
`verifiedBootState` genuinely `Verified`, because a genuine locked device really did produce it.
Checks 1 through 8 all pass honestly. The chain simply belongs to a different app on a different
phone — in their demo, `com.quarkslab.attestation.server`. Their note on why it worked is one
sentence: "Our backend never reads it." Guardsquare described the same relay shape, over the
network, on 27 January 2026.

Their conclusion is the reason this chapter exists:

> "Closing the gap does not call for better silicon. It calls for the backend to bind the
> attestation to the app that asked. That check is cheap."

Which also reframes your `attestationApplicationId` check. Its headline use is detecting
repackaging. Its *structural* job is **binding the attestation to the asker** — stopping a
genuine attestation from a different app or device being presented as yours. That second job is
the one a naive verifier silently skips, and it's the one that holds against an attacker who
never breaks any crypto at all.

## 11.10 Key attestation or Play Integrity?

File 10 presented Play Integrity as the mainstream path. Having seen the machinery, the trade is
clearer:

| | **Key attestation** | **Play Integrity** |
|---|---|---|
| You verify | an X.509 chain + extension, yourself | a token, by calling Google's servers |
| Needs Google Play services on the device | **no** — plain AOSP Keystore | yes |
| Available | **every device launched with Android 8.0+** — attestation wasn't *required* before API 26, so pre-8.0 devices may lack the hardware support ([developer.android.com](https://developer.android.com/privacy-and-security/security-key-attestation)) | GMS devices |
| Binds a **key** you can then use per-request | **yes** — this is its real advantage | no |
| Gives you app licensing / Play-recognised-binary verdicts | no | yes |
| Effort and ways to get it wrong | high — §11.7 is all yours | low |

The GMS row is the deciding one for anything that must work on devices without Google services
— de-Googled ROMs, some regions, some OEMs, and embedded or kiosk builds. File 10 listed that
population as a standing cost of Play Integrity; key attestation is the mechanism that doesn't
pay it. The price is that every check in §11.7 is yours to get right, which is why starting from
[`github.com/android/keyattestation`](https://github.com/android/keyattestation) rather than a
hand-rolled parser is the right default — with the caveats in §11.5 and §11.8 configured
deliberately, because its defaults are looser than its documentation's advice.
